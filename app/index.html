The Riemann Hypothesis

One statement about where a function vanishes, and three generalisations that stretch it across all of arithmetic. Each comes with a live picture of its L-function — over the complex plane, where the zeros are white points strung along a single vertical line, or along that line itself, where they are crossings.

Where this starts

For s a complex number with real part greater than 1, the series ∑ n−s converges, and Euler's identity rewrites it as a product over the primes. That single identity is why a question about primes can be asked as a question about a function of a complex variable. Riemann's 1859 memoir continued the function to the whole plane and observed that the distribution of primes is governed by the location of its zeros.

All the interesting zeros lie in the critical strip, and the hypothesis is that they lie on a single line down its middle. It is unproved. It is also not one conjecture but a family. The same statement can be made for the zeta function of a number field (the Extended Riemann Hypothesis), for a Dirichlet L-function (the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis), and for the L-function of an elliptic curve or any automorphic form (the Grand Riemann Hypothesis). The names are not used entirely consistently in the literature, and the four statements are not independent — but they get harder in roughly this order. Pick one above.

Variant one

The Riemann zeta function

The conjecture

Every zero of ζ(s) in the strip 0 < Re(s) < 1 satisfies Re(s) = ½.

a

The L-function

Defined first by a series that only converges on a half-plane, then extended everywhere else by analytic continuation — the continued function is what the hypothesis is about.

ζ(s) = ∑n=1∞ 1 / ns valid for Re(s) > 1

The continuation is holomorphic on all of ℂ except for a single simple pole at s = 1 with residue 1. That pole is the analytic shadow of the fact that ∑ 1/p diverges — there are infinitely many primes.

b

Coefficients

The most featureless coefficients possible: every one of them is 1.

Everything arithmetic in ζ comes from the exponent n−s, not from the numerators. The other two variants earn their difficulty by putting real arithmetic into these coefficients.

c

Euler product

ζ(s) = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1 valid for Re(s) > 1

Expanding each factor as a geometric series and multiplying out reproduces ∑ n−s exactly once per integer — the product is unique factorisation, written analytically.

d

The critical strip

The Euler product shows there are no zeros with Re(s) > 1. The functional equation reflects that half-plane onto Re(s) < 0, where the only zeros are the trivial ones at s = −2, −4, −6, … Everything unresolved is squeezed into the strip between.

Λ(s) = π−s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s) = Λ(1 − s) the functional equation
e

Zeros in the strip

Imaginary parts of the first zeros above the real axis; each has a mirror image below. These are read off Odlyzko's table, which carries them to 1000 decimal places.

What is known

  • Infinitely many zeros are on the line. Hardy, 1914. This does not rule out infinitely many off it.
  • A positive proportion are on the line. Levinson (1974) got at least a third; Conrey (1989) two fifths; Pratt, Robles, Zaharescu and Zeindler (2020) currently hold the record at more than five twelfths.
  • No zeros on Re(s) = 1. Proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin in 1896, and equivalent to the prime number theorem. The best known zero-free region is still essentially Vinogradov–Korobov, and is far from the line.
  • Verified very far up. Platt and Trudgian (2021) confirmed that every zero with imaginary part below 3 × 1012 lies on the line.
  • It is worth a million dollars. One of the seven Clay Millennium Problems, and the only one also on Hilbert's 1900 list.

The hypothesis is equivalent to a sharp error term in the prime counting function: π(x) = li(x) + O(√x log x). Loosely, RH says the primes are as evenly distributed as they could possibly be.

Further reading

  • B. Riemann, Über die Anzahl der Primzahlen unter einer gegebenen Grösse (1859). The original eight pages.
  • H. M. Edwards, Riemann's Zeta Function (1974). Still the best way in; builds everything from the memoir.
  • J. B. Conrey, The Riemann Hypothesis, Notices of the AMS 50 (2003), 341–353. The standard survey.
  • D. Platt and T. Trudgian, The Riemann hypothesis is true up to 3·1012, Bull. LMS 53 (2021). The current verification.
  • K. Pratt, N. Robles, A. Zaharescu, D. Zeindler, More than five-twelfths of the zeros of ζ are on the critical line, Res. Math. Sci. 7 (2020). The current proportion.

Variant two

Dedekind zeta functions

The conjecture

For every number field K, every zero of ζK(s) in the strip 0 < Re(s) < 1 satisfies Re(s) = ½.

Replace ℤ by the ring of integers of a number field and the whole construction goes through — with one complication that is the reason to care. In a general ring of integers, numbers do not factor uniquely. The example here is K = ℚ(√−5), the smallest field where that failure is visible.

6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) two genuinely different factorisations into irreducibles
a

The L-function

Kummer and Dedekind's repair was to factor ideals instead of numbers, and for ideals factorisation is unique again. So the zeta function of K counts ideals, graded by their norm.

ζK(s) = ∑𝚸 ≠ 0 1 / N(𝚸)s over non-zero ideals of the ring of integers, Re(s) > 1

How far unique factorisation fails is measured by the class number h, the size of the group of ideals modulo principal ones. For ℚ(√−5), h = 2; for ℚ(√−23), h = 3; for ℚ(√−3), h = 1 and factorisation does work. All three are available in the plot below.

b

Coefficients

Here an counts the ideals of norm exactly n. A zero means no ideal has that norm.

a11 = 0 because 11 stays prime in ℤ[√−5]: the only ideal above it has norm 11² = 121. a3 = 2 because 3 splits into two distinct primes, each of norm 3. a2 = 1 because 2 ramifies — the prime (2, 1+√−5) appears squared.

c

Euler product

ζK(s) = ∏𝚹 prime (1 − N(𝚹)−s)−1 now over prime ideals, not prime numbers

Grouping the prime ideals by the rational prime underneath them gives the identity that makes this variant computable. Each rational prime either splits into two primes of norm p, stays inert as a single prime of norm p2, or ramifies — and which one happens is recorded by the quadratic character χD of the discriminant.

ζK(s) = ζ(s) · L(s, χD) for any quadratic field of discriminant D

This is worth pausing on, because it settles what ERH does and does not add. For an abelian field, the Dedekind zeta function is a product of Dirichlet L-functions, so ERH for it is exactly RH together with GRH for those factors — no new content. The real weight of ERH sits on the non-abelian fields, where the factors are Artin L-functions that are not even known to be entire.

d

The critical strip

Same strip, same line. For an imaginary quadratic field the completed function carries the discriminant as its conductor and a single Γ-factor, and the trivial zeros fill in all the negative integers — the even ones inherited from ζ and the odd ones from L(s, χD).

ΛK(s) = |dK|s/2 (2π)−s Γ(s) ζK(s) = ΛK(1 − s) imaginary quadratic: one complex place, no real ones

There is still a simple pole at s = 1, and its residue is not an accident — it is the analytic class number formula, which ties the analysis directly to the arithmetic the field started with.

lims→1 (s − 1) ζK(s) = 2πh / (w√|dK|) h the class number, w the roots of unity

Because ζK = ζ · L(s, χD), the zeros are the two lists interleaved. Below they are shown separately so you can see which is which; in the plot they are one set of points on one line.

What is known

  • The class number formula is a theorem. Dirichlet for quadratic fields, Dedekind in general. It is what turns L(1, χD) into an arithmetic quantity: for ℚ(√−5) it equals π/√5 = 1.404962946…, and the 2 hiding inside it is the class number.
  • The quotient is entire. Aramata and Brauer proved that ζK / ζ is entire whenever K/ℚ is Galois. For non-Galois K this is still open in general — it is Dedekind's conjecture, and it follows from Artin's holomorphy conjecture.
  • ERH makes class groups computable. Bach proved that under ERH the class group is generated by the prime ideals of norm at most 12(log |dK|)². Unconditionally one falls back on the Minkowski bound, which is enormously larger. This is not a footnote: it is why serious class group computations are usually reported as conditional on ERH.
  • And makes Chebotarev effective. Lagarias and Odlyzko's effective Chebotarev density theorem, under ERH, bounds the norm of the smallest prime with a given Frobenius. Without ERH the bounds are ineffective in the same way Siegel's are.
  • Verified numerically, field by field. As with ζ there is no proof, only computation: LMFDB stores verified zeros for a large number of number fields.

Further reading

  • J. Neukirch, Algebraic Number Theory, ch. VII. Dedekind zeta, the functional equation, the class number formula.
  • E. Bach, Explicit bounds for primality testing and related problems, Math. Comp. 55 (1990). The 12(log|d|)² bound.
  • J. Lagarias and A. Odlyzko, Effective versions of the Chebotarev density theorem (1977). What ERH buys you.
  • H. Aramata (1933) and R. Brauer (1947). ζK/ζ is entire for Galois K.
  • LMFDB: the field ℚ(√−5) and ℚ(√−23). Class groups, and the zeros used here.

Variant two

Dirichlet L-functions

The conjecture

For every Dirichlet character χ, every zero of L(s, χ) in the strip 0 < Re(s) < 1 satisfies Re(s) = ½.

Where ζ counts all integers equally, these functions sort them by remainder. The example here is the quadratic character of conductor 3, the one attached to the field ℚ(√−3).

a

The L-function

Let χ be the non-trivial character modulo 3: it sends n to +1 when n ≡ 1, to −1 when n ≡ 2, and to 0 when 3 divides n. It is the Kronecker symbol (−3 / ·), and it is odd: χ(−1) = −1.

L(s, χ) = ∑n=1∞ χ(n) / ns valid for Re(s) > 1

Because χ is non-trivial the partial sums stay bounded, so this one is entire — no pole at s = 1, and that is exactly what makes the next fact possible.

b

Coefficients

Periodic with period 3, and that is the whole of it.

c

Euler product

L(s, χ) = ∏p ≠ 3 (1 − χ(p) p−s)−1 valid for Re(s) > 1

A prime p > 3 contributes a factor that depends only on whether p is 1 or 2 mod 3 — equivalently, by quadratic reciprocity, on whether −3 is a square mod p, which is whether p splits in ℚ(√−3).

d

The critical strip

Same strip, same line, a different gamma factor: because χ is odd the completed function uses (s+1)/2 rather than s/2, which moves the trivial zeros to the negative odd integers −1, −3, −5, …

Λ(s, χ) = (3/π)(s+1)/2 Γ((s+1)/2) L(s, χ) = Λ(1 − s, χ) root number +1 for this character
e

Zeros in the strip

Computed in Magma and agreeing with LMFDB's entry 1-3-3.2-r1-0-0 to every digit it prints.

f

The function on the plane

The other two options are the characters of conductor 20 and 23 — the ones attached to the two fields in the ERH panel. Notice how much lower the first zero sits as the conductor grows: 8.04 for conductor 3, 2.36 for conductor 20, 2.87 for conductor 23. Zeros are packed more densely when the conductor is larger.

g

What is known

  • L(1, χ) ≠ 0. Dirichlet, 1837 — and this non-vanishing is the proof that every arithmetic progression a, a+q, a+2q, … with gcd(a, q) = 1 contains infinitely many primes.
  • Its value is a class number. For this character the analytic class number formula gives L(1, χ) = 2πh / (w√3) = π/(3√3) = 0.604599788…, using h = 1 and w = 6 roots of unity in ℚ(√−3). Hover the plot at s = 1 and you are reading off a class number.
  • Siegel zeros are the gap. Nobody can rule out a single real zero of a real character's L-function sitting very close to s = 1. GRH would kill it instantly; without GRH the best bounds (Siegel's) are ineffective, meaning they contain a constant no one can compute.
  • A great deal is conditional on it. Under GRH the Miller–Rabin test becomes a deterministic polynomial-time primality test (Miller, 1976), Artin's primitive root conjecture holds (Hooley, 1967), and Chebotarev's density theorem becomes effective (Lagarias–Odlyzko).

  • H. Iwaniec and E. Kowalski, Analytic Number Theory, AMS Colloq. Publ. 53 (2004). Chapter 5 for the general framework.
  • C. L. Siegel, Über die Classenzahl quadratischer Zahlkörper (1935). The ineffective bound.

Variant three

Automorphic L-functions

The conjecture

For every cuspidal automorphic representation of GLn over a number field, every non-trivial zero of its L-function lies on the central line.

This is the version that swallows the other two: ζ is the GL1 case over ℚ, Dirichlet L-functions are the other GL1 cases, and an elliptic curve gives a GL2 case. The example here is the curve of conductor 11 — the smallest conductor there is.

A warning about normalisation. For an elliptic curve the natural variable puts the centre at s = 1 and the strip at ½ < Re(s) < 3/2; this is what the plot below uses. Replacing s by s + ½ slides everything back to the familiar picture with the line at ½. Both conventions are in constant use.

a

The L-function

Take the elliptic curve 11a1 (LMFDB 11.a2), the curve y2 + y = x3 − x2 − 10x − 20, of conductor 11. For each prime of good reduction count its points mod p and record the deficit.

ap = p + 1 − #E(𝔽p),    L(E, s) = ∑n=1∞ an / ns valid for Re(s) > 3/2

That the series continues to the whole plane at all is the modularity theorem: L(E, s) equals the L-function of a weight-2 newform of level 11. This is the hardest input on the page, and it was only proved for all elliptic curves over ℚ in 2001.

b

Coefficients

Genuinely arithmetic, and no longer bounded: Hasse's theorem pins |ap| ≤ 2√p, and how they fluctuate inside that range is itself a deep question (Sato–Tate).

c

Euler product

L(E, s) = ∏p ∤ N (1 − app−s + p1−2s)−1 ∏p | N (1 − app−s)−1 degree 2 at the good primes, degree 1 at the bad ones

The quadratic factor is what “degree 2” means, and it is why the zeros are packed about twice as densely as ζ's at the same height.

d

The critical strip

Λ(s) = Ns/2 (2π)−s Γ(s) L(E, s) = ε Λ(2 − s) ε = +1 for 11a1, −1 for 37a1

The sign ε is forced by the curve and it decides the parity of the order of vanishing at the centre. When ε = −1 the completed function is odd about s = 1 and therefore must vanish there — which is why 37a1 has a zero sitting exactly at the centre and 11a1 does not.

What is known

  • The functions exist. For elliptic curves over ℚ, analytic continuation and the functional equation follow from modularity: Wiles and Taylor–Wiles (1995) for the semistable case, completed by Breuil, Conrad, Diamond and Taylor (2001). For general automorphic L-functions this is Godement–Jacquet.
  • The centre is understood in low rank. Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer predict that the order of vanishing at s = 1 equals the rank of E(ℚ). When the analytic order is 0 or 1 this is a theorem — Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin. 11a1 has rank 0 and L(E, 1) = 0.2538418608…; 37a1 has rank 1 and vanishes there.
  • No zero-free region reaching the line. As with ζ, non-vanishing on Re(s) = 3/2 is known and everything inside is open.
  • The statistics look right. Spacings between high zeros match the eigenvalue statistics of random unitary matrices, and low-lying zeros in families follow the Katz–Sarnak predictions — evidence of the spectral interpretation that a proof would presumably need.
  • Maass forms are the other GL2 case. For a Maass cusp form on the modular surface, Grand RH is equally open, and it sits beside a second unproved statement about the same objects: the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture, where the best bound is Kim–Sarnak's 7/64. Those L-functions need spectral parameters that this page does not carry, so the live example here stays with the elliptic curves.
